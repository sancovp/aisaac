# Low-ticket digital product: the five tests

A low-ticket digital product is the IMPULSE purchase that removes one recurring pain, never the
considered core purchase. Tom's metaphor: the triathlete deliberates for months over the bike, the
wheel and the helmet. He buys the $40 chamois cream on the spot, and keeps buying it, because riding
without it hurts. Sell the cream, not the bike.

## Score every candidate 1–10 on each test (each test has a failure end and a success end)

| # | test | the question | low score gives you | high score gives you |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | PAIN | how much does the problem hurt, right now? | no desire | people go looking for it |
| 2 | SINGULAR | does it solve exactly one problem? | a weak product that keeps no promise | an excellent product that keeps its one promise |
| 3 | DAILY | will the buyer use it every day (or every session)? | bought once, forgotten, never mentioned | a habit, so buyers recommend it unprompted |
| 4 | PROVEN | do you (or others) already use or sell something like it? | you are inventing from scratch | easy to make: repackage what works |
| 5 | SHOWABLE | can a viewer SEE it working in a piece of content? | you cannot sell it | every video is a demo |

Rule for test 5: **if you cannot show it, you cannot sell it at low ticket.** A tool that is hard
to explain (a complex workflow, an agent system, a hard-to-explain sheet) can still be valuable for
clients, members or your own work, but it is not a low-ticket digital product.

Tom's daily-use proof: a $9 net-worth sheet (Compiled Sanity) that he has filled in every month
for years, and has sent about a thousand buyers to. Cheap, singular, a habit, and visible.

## The content-to-sales loop

```
content (names a problem) → audience (drawn by that problem) → purchase
   → FINISH LINE (the buyer reaches the promised result)
   → content about that before/after → (loops back to content)
```

People pay for the finish-line MOMENT, not for the gear. Tom's $20,000 Ironman was paid for the
finisher photo and the announcer calling his name. So the product needs a visible finish line a
buyer can picture themselves crossing, and every finish line becomes the next piece of content.

## Price it, build it, send traffic to it (Maria Wendt — `docs/maria-wendt-mechanism.md`)

- **The price gate.** An impulse buy lives below $97: $27–57 is the sweet spot, $27 the no-brainer start.
  $197–997 is the DEAD ZONE: too much to buy on impulse, too little to pay for the sales infrastructure
  (calls, objections) a considered buy needs. If it does not sell at $27, the problem is never the price —
  it is the packaging, the message or the traffic.
- **The ×10 test.** A good low-ticket product could sell at ten times its price; the gap is the point.
- **Build in 2–5 hours, set up in 2 days, send traffic for 28.** One hyper-specific problem, never an
  academy. A rough first version is fixed by its first hundred buyers, who say what is wrong.
- **Straight to the checkout.** The content's call to action goes to the checkout page (a comment keyword
  that DMs one link is the simplest form); no email capture, nurture sequence or webinar in front of a
  low-ticket sale — it cools the buyer. Email the CUSTOMERS afterwards: repeat purchases are where it pays.
- **Measure each piece by the money it makes**, with its own keyword, never by its views.

## How to run it

1. List the candidates. Score each on the five tests IN WRITING, with one line of evidence per score.
2. Drop anything below ~7 on PAIN or SHOWABLE. Those two kill a product outright.
3. Split anything that fails SINGULAR into its separate problems and score each one again.
4. For the winner, name the finish line (the moment, as a picture) and the first three pieces of
   content: one showing the pain, one showing the product being used, one showing a before/after.
5. Offers are *USER*'s (`.claude/rules/00-SITE-CANON.md` HARD LAW 1): this skill picks and argues
   for a product. It never puts a price or a product on a page.
