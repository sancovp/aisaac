The longer version

Pick one job in your business that happens over and over. If I asked you to draw it right now, you’d get two or three steps in and it would go fuzzy. That isn’t a memory problem — the steps are real, nobody ever wrote them down, so they live inside whoever has been there longest.

Which means you can’t require anyone to follow them, because you can’t require what isn’t written. Which means there’s no reward structure attached to them, so nobody is measured on them. Which means the culture is made of something other than the work — and that’s where the drama and the politics come from. It also means nothing is emitting data, so you have no visibility, so you're guessing about which parts of your own company actually make the money. When someone quits, the process walks out with them. When you try to automate something, it stalls, because there was never anything solid to automate.

Right?

Now picture that same job mapped. Receive, then review, then check, then approve. Four boxes anyone in the room can look at and argue with. The moment you can see it, you can ask which of those four steps is worth replacing — and you replace only those. That’s the whole mechanism and there’s nothing clever hiding behind it: your business has to know what it is doing if you want AI to know what to do in your business.

And once a process is written down it is data, and data can be compiled into intelligence. So you don’t do this alphabetically — you do the most profitable box first. Each one you convert changes what’s possible for the ones next to it, because their inputs just became clean. It compounds. The goal was never more AI. The goal is a more capable business.

Right?

So what you actually need is an AI CTO — someone who does this for a living — running a framework instead of opinions:

  • Map — every department, every role, every process, and whether there’s a written procedure behind each one. The holes are the finding.
  • Prioritize — which of those touch revenue, which hurt the most, which take the most time, and which frankly shouldn’t exist at all.
  • Use what exists — research what already solves it, because most of the time something already does and buying beats building.
  • Implement — keep the step, buy something, configure it, connect two things you already own, or put AI on it. Whichever is actually right.
  • Customize — and only where the market genuinely has nothing that fits, build the piece that’s missing.

Inside every one of those, the same loop runs: discover, map, analyze, research, decide — and decide has three real answers, which are map more, implement, or stop. Each pass produces a clearer map and a better next decision.

A consultant charges you to study this and hand you a deck, and then you’re holding a document and a bill. Meanwhile the thing you were going to do instead — put your own team on it, try tools until something sticks, pay someone to wire it up, and lose the months in between — costs more than any of it, and you can’t see that cost because it’s spread across everyone’s week.

Fewer bad bets, faster wins, and a business that gets progressively more AI-integrated instead of one that bought some AI.
