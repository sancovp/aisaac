The Altitude Ladder

There's a ladder, it shows up twice, and most people are lower on it than they think. The most honest move on it is usually "don't climb."

The shape

There's a ladder of altitude, and the same ladder shows up two ways:

  • As engineering capability: say-the-right-thing → give-it-actions → give-it-knowledge → put-a-system-around-it → make-it-provably-correct → keep-it-correct-over-time → make-the-whole-loop-improve-itself.
  • As economic position: sell-your-time → resell-one-thing → combine-many-things → make-the-thing- others-resell → own-the-platform-they-all-run-on → be the compiler that generates all of the above.

Three things are true about this ladder:

  1. Most people are far lower than they think, and each rung needs a discipline the one below didn't — you can't reach a high rung by doing more of a low one.
  2. The low rungs are now trivially automatable. The tasks that whole industries charge a fortune for have collapsed to minutes of work; the markup was always rent on a knowledge gap, and that gap is closing.
  3. The top rung is not a product — it's a person. The very top is someone who has internalized the whole ladder and forms a team (with their AI) that produces capability nobody designed in advance. You can buy the rungs. The top you either become or keep on retainer. There is no third option, because you cannot automate the automator.

Why it matters

This is the map that lets you stop getting scammed and stop scamming yourself. Almost the entire AI services industry sells a low rung while pricing it like a high one — and the pretense only works while you can't see the board. Seeing the board changes everything: you know what a given offer actually is, you know the markup is on a gap you could close, and you know which rung you genuinely need.

The most counterintuitive part — and the most honest — is that the right move is often "don't climb." A clean low rung doing a simple job well is a real position, not a failure. The mistake is climbing past where your stakes require because a blog post made you feel like you should; the cost of the upper rungs is enormous and you only pay it when the business case forces you. Honesty about which rung you need is itself the differentiator, because everyone else is selling the same high rung to everyone.

How to think with it

  1. Locate yourself honestly — on both ladders. What rung is the thing you're doing (or buying) actually on? Not what it's branded as.
  2. For anything you're paying for, ask: is the price on the work, or on a knowledge gap I could close myself? If it's the gap, you have two cheaper options: close it, or jump over it deliberately.
  3. Ask the stakes question: what does a wrong answer cost? If it's an apology email, you may belong on a low rung and should stay there. If it's five figures, a customer, or a life, the upper rungs stop being optional.
  4. Remember the top is a relationship, not a deliverable. If you need the top, you're choosing between becoming that person for your domain or retaining one — not buying a feature.

Honest caveat. What this doesn't cover: the path up. This is the map, deliberately — most people who read it correctly will conclude they need to buy nothing, which is the point of publishing it.
