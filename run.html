The part that makes it hold up

A logic layer checks the work against your rules before it acts.

Most automation runs a task and hopes. This layer sits between an agent deciding and an agent acting: your business rules, written down formally, consulted first. It is the reason output holds up instead of quietly drifting.

01

Your rules, written down

How your business actually works, formalised into something a machine can check against — so the agent operates inside boundaries you set instead of guessing at them.

02

Checked before it runs

Every action is tested against those rules before execution, so an out-of-bounds action is caught in front of the work rather than patched behind it.

03

Conclusions it can show its work for

The system derives results from your rules and can say which rule produced which conclusion, so a surprise is traceable instead of mysterious.

04

No agent marks its own homework

Work is graded by something other than the thing that produced it, and when the rules change the checks change with them.

Generative R&D

We map every product you could already sell.

Everything you sell is a combination of options you already own — and you are selling the handful of combinations history happened to hand you. We model your option space, enumerate it, certify what you already claim, and hand you the map of the rest. When the market moves, you answer with a lookup while a competitor schedules a product-development meeting.

The worked demonstration: a famous burger menu has 972 valid combinations. The published menu claims 3. The secret menu — the combinations customers found on their own — is the proof that the market pays for the dark region. Read the demonstration, with the enumeration shown →

“Can’t ChatGPT do this?” It can count your combinations once, in a chat that forgets. It cannot hold the space as a navigable, certified map that composes across your products, pricing and marketing and stays organised on its own. That substrate is the technology; the frontier models work inside it.
